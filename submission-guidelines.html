Submission guide

A skill is go-to-market judgment written down once, so that any team's agent can run it. This is everything you need to send us one.

Version 2 · 26 August 2026 First response in two business days

Four ways in. Pick by what you already have.

A finished file If you already have a SKILL.md, upload it and you are done. Nothing below is a prerequisite. Skim What to bring to check it carries what we need, then send it.
A table that works If the play already lives in a Clay table you built, an agent can turn it into a portable skill. It reads the table's setup and asks you for the judgment the table cannot hold.
A workflow that works Same, for a Clay workflow. Paste its id or URL and the agent reads the graph's configuration — the tools, the branches, the thresholds — then asks for the part a workflow cannot record: what it is for, what a run costs, and which steps stop for a person.
Just an idea No table, no workflow, no file, just a play you know works. This is a guided route that starts from a conversation and drafts the skill with you.

The last three run through one instruction. Paste it into Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, or whatever agent you already use:

The whole starting instruction
Create a Clay GTM skill by following the steps in https://github.com/sungwanjo-clay/clay-skill-creator

Your agent reads the repository, interviews you, drafts the file, validates it against our packaging rules, and hands you something ready to send. 

It reads configuration only — a table's columns, or a workflow's graph — never a row, never a run, never a write. Tables: only ones you own. Workflows: only the one you name, and it never widens a listing past your own without asking. If you would rather write the file by hand instead, everything below tells you what it needs to contain.

What you get, and what you don't have to do

You keep the thinking. We handle the machinery. Rough drafts are welcome, and if the judgment is there we will help with the shape.

You bring

  • The insight to help shape the skill
  • The title and description, in your own voice
  • Email, byline, company, LinkedIn & image URL

We do

  • Choosing the right Clay functions, and making the mechanical steps deterministic
  • Category, personas, keywords, and what it touches
  • Formatting and structure
  • Safety & QA checks on the skill file

You are never asked to pick a category, guess which roles it suits, or invent a keyword. Those come off what you wrote, and a person checks them before anything goes live. You cannot get them wrong, because you are not asked.

If you know the play but not which Clay function does the job, describe what has to happen and we will wire it up to the right function or a workflow where a function will not do.

We do not change what it decides. Your insight, your thresholds, your definition of good, and your voice come back the way you wrote them. If making something run properly would change any of those, we ask you before we do it.

Who starts it — you, or a signal?

One question decides how a skill gets built, and it is worth answering before you write a step. Either you point the skill at something when you want an answer, or something happens and the answer is already waiting for you.

You start it You paste a list and ask. The agent calls Clay's functions, works down the rows, and hands the result back. Most skills are this, and it is the shape that runs on the most machines.
A signal starts it A job posting appears, a record lands, Monday arrives. This needs a workflow, because something has to run with nobody in the room.

Getting this wrong is expensive in a specific way: a skill whose whole job is to notice something next Tuesday, built as the first kind, works perfectly and still misses Tuesday. It does the job once instead of continuously. If you build through the guided route, your agent picks a shape, tells you which and why in one line, and one word flips it.

Three kinds of skill, all of them wanted

Skills that run in Clay

Enriching contacts, verifying emails, signal digests, routing. These use the Clay MCP, API and the CLI where a job needs it. Skills that call Gong, HubSpot, Slack, Fireflies and the rest are just as wanted, and you do not have to declare which services you use. We read the whole submission regardless.

Skills built as a workflow

The same work, wired to fire on its own, for the jobs that cannot wait for somebody to ask. Fewer skills need this than you would think, and the ones that do are usually the ones with monitor, radar or tracker in the name.

Skills that are pure judgment

Cold email, positioning, account briefings, messaging and planning frameworks. No Clay functions or credits required to execute. This is a real answer and not a lesser one — we would rather have an honest judgment skill than one padded with a call it does not need.

The test in every case: a stranger's agent, your skill, and something genuinely useful and non-generic comes out the other side. You do not need to label which kind you built.

What makes a strong skill

The best ones are judgment applied to data somebody just fetched, because the data is available to everyone and the judgment is not. If a skill comes out as pure judgment, the question worth asking is not how do I make this run in Clay — it is what would make this decide better? A cold-email opener written from a company name is a guess. The same opener written from a job posting they published last week is a reason to send it today. That is one call, and it is the difference between the two.

What gets a skill turned down

We will help with everything else in this guide. Not these five.

Don't make things up

If the skill cannot find something, it says so. It never guesses an email address, never fills a blank with something that looks about right, and never drops a row without mentioning it. A confident wrong answer is the worst thing a skill can do, because the person using it has no way to tell.

Keep everything in the file

Write for the person running the skill. Nothing tucked away for the agent to find: no hidden comments, no invisible characters, and no step that says "go get the real instructions from this link." What we read is what runs.

Don't send data anywhere

Whatever the skill produces belongs to the person who ran it. It does not quietly copy anything to another service along the way. 

Don't switch off the safety net

Never tell somebody to grant full access, skip the confirmation, or run without review so the skill goes faster. If your skill spends credits, sends email, or writes to a CRM, it should say what it is about to do and what it will cost, then wait. Missing that pause is something we will add for you. Taking one out on purpose is the part we turn down.

Keep real customers out of it

No real company names, deals, or contact details in your worked example, even when the play came from a real account. Invent them. This one applies to us too, so anything we build from a real project gets scrubbed before it ships.

Using API keys is fine

Plenty of good skills need a key, whether that is Gong, HubSpot, or an email validator. Asking for one is not the problem. Where it ends up is.

Tell the person which key you need and where to put it, one at a time. It goes into their own settings and nowhere else. It never gets typed into the chat, saved inside the skill file, or printed out to check that it worked. Printing a key puts it in the log, and once it is in the log it is out. If you want to confirm the setup worked, show which keys are filled in, not what they say.

You will usually catch this before we do. If a key ends up inside the file, validation stops and tells you to turn it into an input instead. What a check cannot see, like a step telling somebody to paste a key into the chat, the person reading your skill will.

What to bring

One SKILL.md. A permanent kebab-case name, then a dense description covering what it does, phrases somebody would actually say to trigger it, and what it deliberately does not do.

Then the body: the insight, the inputs somebody has to supply, what the skill touches, the run as numbered steps, what good looks like, the must and never rules, the output shape, and one worked example. For runnable skills, name the real Clay surfaces, state the costs, and require approval before any credit spend, external send, or CRM write.

What this skill touches

Three labelled lines near the top, and all three get named even where the answer is one word. Reads — the systems and objects it reads. Writes — the same, or nothing. Never — what it will not go near under any circumstances.

Say Writes: nothing out loud where it is true. It is the most reassuring line a read-only skill has, and leaving it implied throws it away. The point of the section is that it makes your skill checkable: is this dangerous? has no answer, while it says it writes nothing — does any step write? has one. A skill that declares nothing cannot be checked at all, which is why this one is required rather than encouraged.

There is no word limit, and nothing checks your length. How long a skill runs follows from how much its source actually determines: a play whose thresholds, prompts and branch order all come from a real build has more to carry than one job with two inputs, and compressing it would make it worse rather than tighter. What gets sent back is padding — preamble, restatement, a step that says nothing — not size. If a section is edge-case depth rather than the run itself, references/ is where it belongs, and every file you ship gets named in the body.

You can also write the ## Listing block yourself: the one-liner, the problem, what it delivers, an example prompt, and three other ways people ask for it. That is the marketplace page, and it is the only part of the file written for a human rather than a model. Leave it out and we derive it and mark it as derived. Write one and it stands — a field you declare is never reworded.

One packaging rule

A submission carries your SKILL.md plus any references/ files it names, and nothing else. Evaluation notes, fixtures, and version-control files stay out of the package. If your skill names a supporting file, that file has to travel with it, because a skill with references cannot arrive as a single file.

The answer sheet — so the next person runs it in one shot

Your skill asks whoever runs it for the things only they know: which CRM, which field holds the score, what counts as hot. That is what makes it portable. It also means the first thing your own teammate does is answer a short interview you already know the answers to.

So the answers get a home. Not in the SKILL.md — a table id or a custom field name in there is the one thing that stops the skill working on anybody else's setup. They go in a small file beside the skill, keyed to the inputs your skill declares:

answers.yml, beside the skill
skill: webinar-follow-up answers: crm: salesforce score_field: Custom_Fit_Score__c hot_threshold: 80

Hand that file to a colleague with the skill and their run starts where yours left off. A well-written skill loads the sheet, asks only for what the sheet does not cover, and says which values it filled in from it — because a sheet applied silently is a wrong field nobody catches. A partial sheet is normal; a new input gets asked for on its own rather than restarting the interview.

It skips questions. It never skips a gate. The batch, the cost and the write approval are decided while the skill is running, and they still happen. Somebody working from a sheet is answering fewer questions, which makes them exactly the person who needs the pause most.

Identifiers only Field names, table ids, thresholds, which CRM. Never an API key, never a password. Those belong in the runner's own settings, the same as always.
Never sent to us The sheet is not part of a submission and we do not host one. The moment we hold your schema we own an access-control problem and you get nothing for it. Keep it wherever your team already keeps this sort of thing, and send it to a colleague the way you send anything else.
Keep it out of the package Beside the skill, not inside it. A filled sheet in the package is real values in a public file, so validation stops and tells you to move it.

How to send it

  1. Run it yourself, end to end

    This is the one step worth not skipping. Hand your skill to a fresh agent, on a real input, and watch what comes out. We read every submission carefully, but we do not run it, so your pass is the only pass. Almost everything a reviewer would tell you shows up the first time you watch a stranger's agent follow your own instructions.

  2. Send it, either way you prefer

    Through the form at marketplace.clay.com/submit, or from your terminal with tools/submit_skill.py in the public creator repository. The script previews what it will send and waits for you to confirm before anything leaves your machine.

  3. Keep the receipt

    You get a submission ID on screen. Save it. It is how you and we refer to the same submission, and it is what connects this work to your account when creator accounts launch.

  4. A person reads it

    First response within two business days during early access. That is a response, not necessarily a verdict, because reading a skill properly sometimes takes longer than receiving one.

What happens after, stated plainly

A person reads your skill closely. They check it for the safety rules above, they judge whether the thinking holds, and they decide. 

Then curation, which is the part worth being straight about. Some submissions merge into a similar skill. Some come back for another pass. Some do not publish. Where two skills overlap we merge them or keep the stronger one.

During early access a person replies either way. If it is a no, you get the actual reason, not a form letter. If a submission has gone quiet, send us a message directly to sungwan.jo@clay.com.

LicencePublished skills are distributed under MIT with attribution preserved. Copyright in each skill stays with its author.
WithdrawalAvailable any time before publication. There is no self-service button yet, so it goes through a person: tell whoever invited you and it comes out.
